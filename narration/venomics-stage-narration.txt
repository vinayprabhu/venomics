1. BOTHROPS JARARACA (Brazilian pit viper)

Stage 1: Brazilian pit viper
This is Bothrops jararaca, the Brazilian pit viper. Its venom contains molecules that helped reveal a powerful way to lower blood pressure. The medical breakthrough began by separating this complex secretion into its components, then asking what each component did to the body.

Stage 2: Venom gland and fang
Move toward the head, behind the eye. Here, a venom gland connects through a duct to a hollow fang in the upper jaw. When the snake bites, muscles compress the gland and drive venom through the fang. The photograph shows the anatomy; the animation traces the delivery pathway.

Stage 3: A complex secretion
A droplet of venom carries many different proteins and peptides. Some damage tissues or alter blood clotting. Others affect chemical signals that control circulation. Researchers separated these components and identified peptides that strengthened the action of bradykinin, a signal that can widen blood vessels.

Stage 4: Teprotide
One crucial lead was teprotide, a nine-residue peptide that reproduces a bradykinin-potentiating peptide from this venom. Its first residue is pyroglutamate, a chemically modified amino acid. Teprotide helped establish that inhibiting angiotensin-converting enzyme could lower blood pressure. The shape shown here is a computed conformation.

Stage 5a: Blood-vessel lining
Now follow the connection into the human circulation. We highlight the tiny blood vessels of the lungs, where angiotensin-converting enzyme is abundant on the vessel lining. The molecule entering this scene is captopril, the drug developed from the earlier peptide research. Its action extends beyond the lungs.

Stage 5b: ACE inhibition
Angiotensin-converting enzyme, usually called ACE, helps produce angiotensin two, a signal that constricts blood vessels. Captopril binds at the enzyme's active site and inhibits it. Less angiotensin two is produced, and bradykinin breakdown also decreases. These changes help reduce blood pressure.

Stage 6: Captopril
The result was captopril, a small molecule that can be taken by mouth. Teprotide supplied a biological lead; medicinal chemistry produced a different, practical drug. This pathway shows how studying a venom peptide can reveal a therapeutic target and guide the design of a medicine.


2. HELODERMA SUSPECTUM (Gila monster)

Stage 1: Gila monster
Meet the Gila monster, Heloderma suspectum. This venomous lizard supplied an unexpected lead for treating type two diabetes. Researchers isolated a peptide called exendin four from its venom. That molecule could activate a human receptor involved in controlling blood glucose.

Stage 2: Lower-jaw venom glands
Focus on the lower jaw. The Gila monster has venom glands beneath its lower teeth. Their secretions reach grooved teeth and enter the wound during a sustained bite. The anatomical image locates the glands, while the adjacent animation follows the secretion toward the bite.

Stage 3: Finding an active component
This secretion is a mixture of biologically active molecules. To understand it, researchers isolate individual components and test their effects. Exendin four emerged from that process. Its activity connected a lizard venom molecule to a signalling system already present in the human body.

Stage 4: Exendin-4
Exendin four contains thirty-nine amino acids. It activates the receptor for glucagon-like peptide one, or G L P one, a hormone involved in glucose control. The ribbon follows an experimentally measured peptide backbone. Its shape can change with its surroundings and when it binds a receptor.

Stage 5a: Pancreatic beta cells
Follow the peptide toward the pancreas. Inside this organ, small clusters of cells called islets contain the beta cells that release insulin. Their G L P one receptors provide one important site of action. The zoom locates the tissue before we examine the receptor at molecular scale.

Stage 5b: GLP-1 receptor activation
Watch the peptide bind to the G L P one receptor on a beta cell. Receptor activation increases internal signals, including cyclic A M P, that strengthen insulin secretion when glucose is elevated. This glucose-dependent response helps bring blood sugar down after a meal.

Stage 6: Exenatide
The synthetic drug counterpart of exendin four is exenatide, marketed in the injection pen shown here as Byetta. It is used to improve glucose control in adults with type two diabetes. A molecule discovered in lizard venom became a manufactured peptide medicine.


3. CONUS MAGUS (Magician cone snail)

Stage 1: Magician cone snail
This is Conus magus, the magician cone snail. Adults capture fish using a specialised tooth and a mixture of fast-acting venom molecules. One component, omega-conotoxin M V I I A, became the basis of a medicine for severe chronic pain.

Stage 2: Venom duct and radular tooth
Inside the snail, an elongated venom gland, often called the venom duct, produces its secretion. The delivery apparatus loads venom into a specialised radular tooth carried by the proboscis. The tissue image here comes from a juvenile; the schematic explains the route toward the tooth.

Stage 3: A changing venom repertoire
Cone-snail venom is a mixture of peptides with different molecular targets. In this species, its composition changes as young snails grow and switch from hunting worms to hunting fish. Separating and studying these components revealed molecules with powerful effects on nerve signalling.

Stage 4: Omega-conotoxin MVIIA
Omega-conotoxin M V I I A is a compact peptide containing twenty-five amino acids. Three disulfide bridges help hold its structure together. The model follows an experimentally measured conformation. A synthetic equivalent of this venom peptide is the drug ziconotide.

Stage 5a: Spinal dorsal horn
The relevant destination is the dorsal horn, at the back of the spinal cord, where incoming sensory nerves relay pain signals. Ziconotide is delivered into the fluid surrounding the spinal cord. The zoom identifies its target region; the bloodstream is not its clinical delivery route.

Stage 5b: Blocking calcium entry
At a sensory nerve ending, ziconotide binds to an N-type calcium channel, called C A V two point two. Blocking calcium entry can reduce the release of chemical messengers that carry pain signals onward. The animation illustrates this process; the peptide blocks the channel from outside the cell.

Stage 6: Ziconotide
Ziconotide is marketed as Prialt for selected patients with severe chronic pain. It requires specialist delivery into spinal fluid and careful monitoring. The therapeutic molecule reproduces a venom peptide, while its formulation and delivery system make that molecule usable as a medicine.


4. APIS MELLIFERA (Western honeybee)

Stage 1: Western honeybee
Meet Apis mellifera, the western honeybee. Its sting can trigger a dangerous allergic reaction in a sensitised person. Yet carefully prepared honeybee venom also has a clinical use: training the immune system to respond less severely to future stings.

Stage 2: Venom sac and stinger
Move to the tip of the worker bee's abdomen. A venom gland supplies a sac connected to the stinger. Two sliding lancets work with a central stylet to penetrate tissue. Together, these structures form the canal that carries venom into the sting wound.

Stage 3: Venom as a mixture
Honeybee venom, also called apitoxin, contains several peptides and proteins. Its clinical allergy treatment uses a prepared venom extract containing multiple components. At this stage, we keep the mixture in view, then select one important allergen to examine at molecular scale.

Stage 4: Api m 1
The molecule shown is Api m one, a major honeybee venom allergen. It is phospholipase A two, an enzyme with one hundred and thirty-four amino acids in this structure. In sensitised people, antibodies can recognise it. Here it represents one component of the therapeutic venom mixture.

Stage 5a: A distributed immune response
Follow the allergen into the context of the human immune system. The spleen is highlighted as an example of lymphoid tissue. Immune tolerance involves cells and tissues throughout the body, so this image does not identify the spleen as a unique treatment target or injection site.

Stage 5b: Reducing allergic reactivity
With medically supervised venom immunotherapy, repeated controlled exposure can change the immune response. Protective antibodies, including I G G four, can intercept allergens. Changes in regulatory signals and immune-cell activity also contribute. The animation shows one part of this broader reduction in allergic reactivity.

Stage 6: Honeybee venom immunotherapy
The clinical product is honeybee venom allergenic extract, used by specialists for venom immunotherapy. It contains a mixture of venom components. The goal is to reduce the risk of serious reactions to future stings. Here, venom itself becomes the material for an allergy treatment.
